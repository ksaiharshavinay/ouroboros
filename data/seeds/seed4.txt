Title: AI in Healthcare: Diagnosis, Access, and Trust (2025)

## Overview
AI is moving from research papers into clinics: reading scans, drafting
notes, triaging messages, and flagging patients at risk. The promise is
real — earlier detection, less paperwork, wider access. So is the risk:
models trained on unrepresentative data, deployed into workflows that
were never designed for them, in a setting where a wrong answer can harm
a person, not just waste a click.

## Key Trends

## 1. Diagnostic AI Reaches the Clinic
Imaging and pathology models now match or exceed human performance on
narrow, well-defined tasks. The open question has shifted from accuracy
in a lab to performance on real patients, real scanners, and real
variation across hospitals.

## 2. The Documentation Burden Moves, Not Vanishes
Ambient scribes and summarisation tools promise to free clinicians from
keyboards. Early adopters report real time savings — and new work:
reviewing, correcting, and taking responsibility for AI-drafted text.

## 3. Bias Travels With the Training Data
Models learn the gaps in the data they are fed: under-represented groups,
under-resourced hospitals, and conditions that present differently across
populations. A model that works well on average can still fail specific
patients systematically.

## 4. Access Widens — Unevenly
AI triage and remote diagnostics can reach places with few specialists,
but only where connectivity, devices, and follow-up care exist. The same
tool can narrow gaps in one setting and widen them in another.

## 5. Regulation and Liability Lag Deployment
Approval frameworks built for fixed medical devices strain against
models that update continuously. Clinicians, hospitals, and vendors are
still negotiating who is liable when an AI recommendation contributes to
a bad outcome.

## Key Actors
- Clinicians (radiologists, pathologists, primary care): Use the tools, bear the liability, and decide when to trust them.
- Patients: Experience the benefits and the errors, often without knowing AI was involved.
- Health systems and insurers: Decide what gets purchased, reimbursed, and integrated into workflows.
- Regulators (FDA and equivalents): Approve devices and set post-market surveillance expectations.
- AI vendors and researchers: Build the models; control much of the training data and evaluation design.

## Emerging Dynamics
- Federated learning is being tried as a way to train on hospital data without moving it.
- Patients are arriving with AI-generated self-diagnoses, changing the consultation itself.
- Ambient documentation is generating vast new text corpora — valuable for research, sensitive for privacy.
- Some systems are publishing model performance broken down by subgroup, turning fairness into a reportable metric.

## Open Questions
1. When a model and a clinician disagree, who should win — and who is responsible either way?
2. How should diagnostic AI be monitored after deployment, when its inputs keep changing?
3. Can AI narrow health disparities, or will it mostly serve the patients and hospitals already best served?
4. What should patients be told about AI's role in their own care?
