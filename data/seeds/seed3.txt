Title: The Energy Transition: Renewables, Grids, and Climate Adaptation (2025)

## Overview
The shift from fossil fuels to renewable energy is no longer mainly a
technology story — solar and wind are now among the cheapest sources of new
electricity in many markets. The hard problems have moved downstream: grids
built for one-way flow from big plants, storage that must cover cloudy and
windless stretches, and communities that experience the transition very
differently depending on where they live.

## Key Trends

## 1. Renewables Scale, Grids Strain
Solar and wind capacity is growing fast, but transmission lines, grid
connections, and permitting move slowly. Projects wait years to connect,
and curtailment — throwing away clean power the grid cannot take — is
becoming common in some regions.

## 2. Storage Becomes the Swing Factor
Batteries are falling in cost and spreading from grid scale to homes and
vehicles. How much storage, of what duration, and who pays for it is now
a central planning question rather than a footnote.

## 3. Electrification of Everything
Heat pumps, electric vehicles, and industrial electrification are shifting
demand onto the power grid even as supply becomes more variable. Managing
when demand happens — through pricing, smart charging, and flexible loads —
matters as much as building new supply.

## 4. Adaptation Joins Mitigation
Heatwaves, floods, and wildfires are forcing cities and insurers to plan
for climate impacts already arriving, not only for emissions cuts. Cooling,
flood defences, and resilient infrastructure compete for the same budgets
as clean-energy build-out.

## 5. The Politics of Who Pays and Who Benefits
Energy transitions create winners (regions with sun, wind, or minerals)
and losers (coal communities, households facing higher bills). Subsidies,
tariffs, and local opposition shape deployment as much as engineering does.

## Key Actors
- Grid operators and utilities: Must keep the lights on while the system is rebuilt around them.
- Governments (national, state, local): Set targets, subsidies, permitting rules, and market design.
- Renewable developers and manufacturers: Build the capacity; exposed to supply chains, interest rates, and trade policy.
- Fossil-fuel companies and workers: Defend existing assets and jobs while some pivot into clean energy.
- Households and communities: Pay the bills, host the infrastructure, and vote on the pace of change.

## Emerging Dynamics
- Vehicle-to-grid and home batteries are turning consumers into small-scale grid participants.
- Long-duration storage (hydrogen, compressed air, thermal) is attracting attention for multi-day gaps batteries cannot cover.
- Climate risk is being priced into insurance and mortgages, quietly redrawing where it is affordable to live.
- Some regions are leapfrogging central grids entirely with solar-plus-storage microgrids.

## Open Questions
1. What matters more for the next decade — building more renewable capacity, or fixing grids and permitting?
2. Who should bear the cost of storage and grid upgrades: ratepayers, taxpayers, or developers?
3. Can adaptation and mitigation be funded together, or will one always crowd out the other?
4. How do communities that lose fossil-fuel jobs get a fair share of the transition's benefits?
