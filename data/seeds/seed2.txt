Title: Governing AI Inside the Enterprise: Data Quality, Privacy, and Accountability (2025)

## Overview
As companies rush to put AI into core operations, the limiting factor is
rarely the model — it is the data underneath it. Poor-quality, undocumented,
or improperly governed data produces confident wrong answers, biased
decisions, and regulatory exposure. Data governance, once a back-office
compliance function, is becoming the control layer that decides whether
enterprise AI is trustworthy enough to use.

## Key Trends

## 1. From Data Quality Checks to AI Readiness
Quality programs are shifting from periodic batch checks to continuous
monitoring of the data that feeds models — freshness, completeness,
lineage, and drift. A dataset that was "good enough" for a dashboard can
fail silently as training or retrieval input.

## 2. Privacy by Design Under Pressure
Regulations in the EU, several US states, and other jurisdictions now
expect purpose limitation, minimization, and deletion rights to be built
into systems, not bolted on. Teams are discovering that retrofitting
privacy into a pipeline built for maximum collection is expensive and
sometimes impossible.

## 3. Lineage and Provenance Become Evidence
When an AI system makes a consequential decision, "which data, from where,
transformed how?" is no longer an audit nicety — it is the explanation.
Lineage graphs, data contracts, and catalogues are moving from
documentation tools to operational evidence.

## 4. The Accountability Gap
Models are owned by one team, data by another, and outcomes by a third.
When an AI decision goes wrong, responsibility is diffuse. Organisations
are experimenting with model owners, AI review boards, and sign-off
rituals, with no settled pattern yet.

## 5. Automation of Governance Itself
AI is now being used to write data-quality rules, classify sensitive data,
and flag anomalies — the same technology being governed is doing the
governing. That speeds coverage but raises the question of who watches
the watcher.

## Key Actors
- Chief Data Officers and data-governance teams: Own the policies, catalogues, and quality programs.
- Regulators (EU AI Act, US state privacy laws, sector regulators): Set the floor for transparency, risk management, and rights.
- Platform and cloud vendors: Ship the catalogues, lineage, and policy engines governance runs on.
- Model builders and data engineers: Live with the controls day to day — and work around them when they slow delivery.
- Auditors and insurers: Increasingly ask for proof that AI systems are governed, not just built.

## Emerging Dynamics
- Data contracts between producers and consumers are spreading as a way to make quality expectations enforceable.
- Synthetic data is being offered as a privacy workaround, raising its own questions about fidelity and hidden bias.
- Small and mid-size companies are adopting governance tooling that was once only affordable at enterprise scale.
- "Governance as code" — policies expressed as executable checks — is narrowing the gap between policy documents and running systems.

## Open Questions
1. Can governance keep pace with how fast teams ship AI features, or does it always become a bottleneck?
2. Who should be accountable when a governed, well-documented system still produces a harmful decision?
3. Does automating governance with AI strengthen oversight, or just move the risk one layer up?
4. What does "good enough" data quality mean when the consumer is a model rather than a person?
